transformer‑from‑scratch: audit the chain rule

I wrote a GPT in NumPy with no autograd, and derived every backward pass by hand. Hand derivations are easy to get subtly wrong, so the model is small enough that every partial derivative can be checked against the definition of a derivative. There are 1,312 of them, and this page checks them in your tab, on tokens you choose.

Model 2 blocks · d=8 · 2 heads Parameters 1,312 in 29 tensors Autograd none Status booting

A backward pass is a claim: change this weight by a little and the loss moves by this much, per unit. That claim is checkable without any calculus at all. Nudge the weight, measure what the loss actually did, and divide. If my algebra was right the two numbers agree; if I dropped a term they don't.

What follows is that check, run on the real package compiled to WebAssembly. Nothing here is precomputed, which is why the audit takes about a second. The first thing I'd like you to do is change the data it runs on, because a page that only ever works on the batch I picked is not evidence of much.

03
Experiment three of six · break it on purpose

Drop a term and see who notices

These are the mistakes I could plausibly have made, and nearly did. Each one is a term left out of a derivation, and each is invisible in the forward pass.

LayerNorm divides by a standard deviation that depends on every coordinate, so differentiating it produces a term in x_hat * sum(d_x_hat * x_hat). Treat the normalisation as a fixed scale and that term never appears. GELU is x·Φ(x), so its derivative is Φ(x) + x·φ(x); read it as a gate on x and you keep only the first half. Both look perfectly reasonable written down, which is the problem.

04
Experiment four of six · the size of the nudge

Why the step is 1e‑5 and not smaller

The difference quotient has two errors pulling opposite ways, and the step that balances them is not the smallest one available.

Truncation error falls like ε² as the step shrinks, because the quotient is a Taylor expansion cut short. Floating-point cancellation grows like η/ε, because subtracting two losses that agree in fifteen digits throws most of them away. Their sum is a V, and I chose ε at the bottom of it.
